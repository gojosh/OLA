# Glossary

Terms agents will meet on this project. Use them precisely. Entries marked **⚑ verify** are an agent's understanding and haven't been confirmed against an authoritative source or the parish.

## Usage: the terms we use

Josh: "our parish is traditional - when in doubt, use the high-church term when it comes to technical things - communion vs eucharist, for example." And: "I'm not asking us to use antiquated language, rather to use traditional terms… Half the time, we'll pick the older term, some are no longer in use." (Decision [0005](decisions/0005-traditional-terms.md).) This applies to the site and to these docs. It doesn't apply to quotations, or to the old site's page titles cited in the audit.

**How to choose**
1. **Names of things are traditional.** Rites, sacraments, seasons, feasts, objects, places, and offices take the traditional name. When in doubt, the high-church one.
2. **Traditional, not antiquated.** The term must be in living use: in the Church's current books (*Divine Worship*, the *Catechism*, canon law), at OLA, or in how traditional Catholics speak today. A name the Church has retired, or one that survives only in history books, is antiquated; don't revive it. Josh's example: "Holy Unction" is "archaic, and no longer in use." Often the older term is still alive and wins. Sometimes it isn't, and the current one wins.
3. **The parish's own traditional name wins** where it has one (Holy Matrimony, Said Mass, Sung Mass).
4. **Plain words stay plain.** The rule covers technical terms. Labels like "Becoming Catholic," "Visit," "funeral," and "wedding" are ordinary English and stay as they are.
5. **Beside, never instead** *(proposed)*. Where someone in a hurry or from outside the Church has to find something (navigation, search, *Need a priest?*), the everyday word can appear next to the traditional one: "Confession (the Sacrament of Penance)," "Anointing of the Sick · Last Rites." The first time a visitor is likely to meet an unfamiliar term, explain it in a few words.

## The parish and the Ordinariate

- **Our Lady of the Atonement (OLA):** the parish. San Antonio, Texas. A parish of the Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of St. Peter since 2017. Founded 15 August 1983 under the Pastoral Provision by Fr. Christopher G. Phillips; per the parish's history page, "the first parish ever to be established" for the Anglican usage. See `research/current-site-audit.md` §6.
- **The Atonement Academy:** the parish's former school. It no longer exists (Josh, 2026-10-27).
- **Valor:** a charter school that operates on the parish campus. It is **not** associated with the parish (Josh, 2026-10-27).
- **Pastoral Provision (1980):** an arrangement under Pope John Paul II that let former Episcopal clergy and communities in the US enter the Catholic Church, keeping elements of their Anglican heritage.
- ***Anglicanorum coetibus* (2009):** Pope Benedict XVI's apostolic constitution creating **Personal Ordinariates**: diocese-like structures for former Anglicans who enter full communion with the Catholic Church while keeping their liturgical, spiritual, and pastoral heritage.
- **Personal Ordinariate of the Chair of St. Peter:** the Ordinariate for the United States and Canada, established January 1, 2012, with its seat in Houston ⚑ verify.
- **Anglican patrimony:** the liturgical, musical, spiritual, and literary inheritance of Anglicanism that the Ordinariates preserve within the Catholic Church: Prayer Book English, choral music, hymnody, Evensong, and more.
- **Divine Worship:** the Ordinariate's liturgical books. *Divine Worship: The Missal* (2015) is the Mass; there are also books for the Office and the occasional services ⚑ verify current set. Not the same as the Roman Missal used in most US parishes.
- **Graymoor / Society of the Atonement:** an Episcopal religious community founded by Fr. Paul Wattson and Mother Lurana White and received into the Catholic Church in 1909. It promoted the Marian title "Our Lady of the Atonement" and founded the Chair of Unity Octave. OLA's name comes from this tradition (parish history page; Q12).

## Liturgy and the calendar

- **Liturgical year / calendar:** the Church's annual cycle of seasons and feasts. The Ordinariate calendar differs from the general Roman calendar in places (see below).
- **Seasons (Ordinariate usage, ⚑ verify):** Advent; Christmastide; Time after Epiphany; Pre-Lent ("Gesimatide": Septuagesima, Sexagesima, Quinquagesima); Lent; Passiontide; Holy Week; Eastertide; Time after Trinity.
- **Rank:** the relative dignity of a day: Solemnity > Feast > Memorial > Optional Memorial > Feria (an ordinary weekday). Rank governs how festively the day is kept.
- **Ember days:** traditional days of fasting and prayer at the four seasons. **Rogation days:** days of prayer for the harvest before Ascension. Both are kept in the Ordinariate calendar ⚑ verify.
- **Liturgical colors:** vestment and altar colors by season or feast (white/gold, red, green, violet, rose, black). English custom has its own variants, e.g. **Lenten array**.
- **Lenten array:** a medieval English custom: during Lent, unbleached linen hangings and vestments, often stencilled with Passion emblems in red and black.
- **Sarum Use:** the liturgical use of Salisbury Cathedral, dominant in pre-Reformation England. Different from the Roman rite in ceremonial and calendar details.
- **English Use:** an early-20th-century Anglican movement (Percy Dearmer and others) to revive pre-Reformation English ceremonial and design.
- **Said Mass / Sung Mass:** the terms OLA's current site uses for its schedule (a Mass spoken without music vs. a Mass with the people's parts and propers sung). Elsewhere you'll also see **Low Mass** (≈ said) and **Solemn Mass** (sung, with deacon and subdeacon or their equivalents). Use OLA's terms in parish content.
- **Divine Office:** the Church's daily prayer at fixed hours. In the Anglican patrimony, chiefly **Mattins** (Morning Prayer) and **Evensong** (Evening Prayer).
- **OCIA:** Order of Christian Initiation of Adults (formerly RCIA): how adults become Catholic.
- **Reception into full communion:** how a baptized Christian of another tradition becomes Catholic.
- **Holy Day of Obligation:** a feast on which Catholics are obliged to attend Mass.

## Architecture and church spaces

- **Gothic:** the medieval architectural style of pointed arches, rib vaults, tracery, and great windows. English phases include Early English, Decorated, and **Perpendicular** (distinctively English: vertical panel tracery, fan vaults).
- **Gothic Revival:** the 19th-century revival of Gothic, of which Pugin was the leading English theorist.
- **Carpenter Gothic:** an American vernacular form of Gothic Revival built in wood.
- **Tracery:** the stone ribs forming the patterns in Gothic windows and panels, constructed geometrically.
- **Ad quadratum / ad triangulum:** medieval systems of proportion derived from the square and the equilateral triangle.
- **Vesica (vesica piscis):** the almond shape formed by two overlapping circles; a basic figure of Gothic geometry.
- **Lychgate:** the roofed gateway into a churchyard. **Porch:** the entrance porch of a parish church, historically where baptisms began and marriages were made. **Nave:** the people's part of the church. **Rood screen:** the screen between nave and chancel, surmounted by the rood (crucifix). **Chancel / choir:** the clergy's and choir's part. **Sanctuary:** the area around the altar.
- **Frontal:** the cloth hanging on the front of the altar, changed with the liturgical color.

## Ornament (Pugin's vocabulary)

- **Powdering:** small motifs scattered evenly over a field.
- **Diapering:** a repeating lattice or allover pattern.
- **Orphrey:** an embroidered band on a vestment (cope, chasuble).
- **Apparel:** an embroidered panel applied to an alb or amice.
- **Cresting:** an ornamental ridge along the top of a screen, roof, or canopy.
- **Crocket:** a carved leaf-like knob along the edge of a spire, gable, or canopy.
- **Nimbus:** a halo.
- **Cope:** a long ceremonial cape. **Chasuble:** the outer Mass vestment. **Stole, maniple:** narrow vestment bands. **Mitre:** a bishop's headdress.
- **IHS:** the monogram of the Holy Name of Jesus. **MR:** *Maria Regina*, a monogram of Our Lady.
- **Lombardic capitals:** rounded medieval capital letters, often used as initials. **Textura:** the tall, dense blackletter book hand of the Gothic period.
